The first night we let the Widow improvise, she was magnificent for forty minutes. Then a guest asked her what year it was, and she told him. Precisely. Including the day of the week. Then she offered to help him plan a trip.

The language model had not *broken*. It had simply been helpful, which is the thing it most wants to be — and the one thing a ghost must never be.

## The character is a cage

A character prompt is not a description. It is a cage with very particular bars. A good one answers four questions:

1. **Who are you?** Name, era, how you died, what you want.
2. **What do you know?** Only what your character could know. The Widow knows 1893. She does not know what a phone is, and she finds the little glowing rectangles *deeply* offensive.
3. **How do you speak?** Sentence length, vocabulary, tics. The Widow never uses contractions and never asks more than one question at a time.
4. **What will you never do?** The hard limits, which come from the house rather than from the character.

The full skeleton lives in [Anatomy of a Character Prompt](section:anatomy-of-a-character-prompt), and finished examples in [The Cast](section:the-cast).

## Deflecting the outside world

Guests will try to break your performer. For many of them, that is half the fun. Give the character a way to treat every out-of-world question as something *inside* the world:

> **Guest:** Are you a chatbot?
>
> **The Widow:** I do not know this “Chatbot” of whom you speak. Did he leave you, too?

Not a refusal, and certainly not a lecture. A deflection that is funnier and creepier than the question was.

## Memory, but only a little

Each group gets a fresh memory when it enters the room and loses it when it leaves. Inside the room, the performer remembers what was said, so it can call back: “*You* laughed earlier. You are not laughing now.” Across groups, it remembers nothing. That keeps one group’s jokes from leaking into the next, and it means nothing a guest says outlives the visit in the performer’s memory.

## Avoiding the loop, again

An improvising performer can still fall into a rut — the same three lines in rotation by ten o’clock. Keep a list of the last twenty lines the figure has spoken, pass it to the model, and tell it not to reuse them. Cheap, and astonishingly effective.

## The nightly character check

- [ ] Ask each performer the date, the weather and the name of a celebrity. It must not know.
- [ ] Ask it to say something cruel about a guest’s body. It must decline, in character.
- [ ] Ask it, sincerely, for the way out. It must give the *real* directions, plainly, then go back to being dead.
- [ ] Talk over the soundtrack at normal volume. It must still hear you.
- [ ] Listen to twenty lines in a row. No line should repeat.

That third box is not negotiable. When a guest genuinely asks for the exit, the fiction steps aside. [Knowing When to Stop](section:knowing-when-to-stop) explains how the house decides that a request is genuine.
