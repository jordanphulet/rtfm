Light, sound and fog are the room’s breath. The animatronic is the face that frightens, but the room is the body it lives in — and a body that breathes out of rhythm is a corpse in the worst possible sense: an unconvincing one.

## Light: what you hide is the show

The rule of haunt lighting is simple: **light only what you want seen, and only for as long as you want it seen.** Everything else is darkness doing its job.

Most rooms use [DMX512](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DMX512), the old, sturdy lighting protocol theatres have trusted for decades. The stage manager sends cue numbers; a small lighting controller turns them into levels. Keep it that way round. Never let a language model set a light level directly. It will discover that blackouts are scary, and it will be right, and a group will be standing in total darkness beside a moving arm.

Two lighting rules I never break:

- **Egress paths are never fully dark.** Dim marker lights along the floor near every exit stay on, whatever the show is doing.
- **Strobes stay slow.** Keep flashing effects at or below three flashes per second, and post a warning at the entrance.[^strobe]

## Sound: the shape of the room

Directional sound is the haunt builder’s ventriloquism. A whisper from a speaker behind the guest’s left ear is worth more than a scream from the ceiling. Run a separate audio zone for every place a sound should seem to come *from*, and route each performer’s voice to a speaker inside or just behind its own body.

The soundtrack and effects are cued over [Open Sound Control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_Sound_Control), which audio software speaks fluently. Performer voices are live audio, routed through the same mixer to each figure’s own speaker. Each room ducks the soundtrack while its performer is listening, as described in [Giving the Dead a Voice](section:giving-the-dead-a-voice).

## Fog: the beautiful liability

Fog makes light visible, hides your mechanisms, and turns a corridor into a dream. It also sets off smoke detectors, hides trip hazards, fools cheap beam-breaks, and irritates the lungs of guests with asthma.

| Effect | Fluid | How it hangs | Risks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fog | Glycol-based | Rises and spreads | Smoke detectors, visibility |
| Haze | Light glycol or mineral oil | Thin, even, long-lasting | Slippery floors over time |
| Low-lying fog | Chilled fog, or dry ice | Hugs the floor | Hides the floor itself |

Use only fluids made for the purpose, keep fog thin wherever people walk, and agree your detector strategy with the fire marshal *in writing* before you open.[^dry-ice] Never, ever let fog swallow an exit sign.

---

When all three are cued by the same stage manager, the room stops being a set and starts being an animal. How hard that animal breathes is decided by [The Director](section:the-director).

[^strobe]: Fast flashing light can trigger seizures in people with [photosensitive epilepsy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosensitive_epilepsy). Three flashes per second is a common safe ceiling.

[^dry-ice]: Dry ice gives off carbon dioxide, which pools in low places and displaces oxygen. Ventilate, and keep it out of any pit, cellar or crawlspace where guests or crew might crouch.
