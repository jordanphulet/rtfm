Every haunt actor learns to read a group in its first three seconds. Who is in front, pretending to be brave. Who is in the middle, clutching a sleeve. Who is at the back, filming. The machine must learn the same art, with much worse eyes.

## Signals a room can actually measure

Everything here comes from the room summary described in [Sensing the Guest](section:sensing-the-guest). No faces, no names.

- **Spacing.** Frightened groups bunch. Confident groups spread out and wander.
  - A tight cluster in the `centre` zone is a rattled group.
  - One person far behind the rest is a straggler. Handle with care.
- **Speed.** Fear makes people either rush or stop dead.
  - Hurrying towards the exit is a good sign the scares are landing.
  - Moving *backwards*, towards the entrance, is a warning.
- **Sound.** Screams followed by laughter are the music of a healthy haunt. Screams followed by silence are not.
- **Talk.** The transcript is a rich signal. “Oh no, oh no, oh no” is delight. “I want to leave” is not, and it is handled by entirely different rules.

## The fear level

The room turns those signals into one number — the group’s **fear level** — on a scale the whole house shares. Don’t confuse it with *intensity*, the push level a group chooses at the ticket desk. Fear level is what the room measures; intensity is what the guests asked for.

| Fear level | Name | What it looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | Strolling | Talking normally, spread out, nobody flinching |
| 1 | Uneasy | Quieter, closer together, glancing around |
| 2 | Rattled | Screams and laughter, bunching, hurrying |
| 3 | Overwhelmed | Silence after screams, freezing, backing up, crying |

Note that **3 is not the goal.** Three is a group that has stopped having fun. A well-tuned haunt keeps most groups swinging between 1 and 2, with brief and glorious peaks.

## How the number is made

1. Each signal contributes a few simple rules, such as “cluster smaller than one metre across: add a point”.
2. The points are summed and smoothed over the last five seconds, so that one shriek does not spike the fear level.
3. A fear level of 3 from *any* room goes straight to that room’s stage manager, bypassing the Director entirely.

That last step is deliberate. The Director is clever and slow; the response to an overwhelmed guest must be dumb and instant. [Knowing When to Stop](section:knowing-when-to-stop) covers what happens next.

> Read the group as a stage actor would: generously. Assume the brave one at the front is the most frightened of all.

I have been wrong about who was scared more often than I have been right.[^wrong] So has every machine I have ever built. Design every rule so that being wrong in the cautious direction is cheap, and being wrong in the other direction is impossible.

[^wrong]: The bravest-looking man I ever frightened sat down, very suddenly, on a foam pad we had placed there for exactly that reason. The smallest child in his group asked to go round again.
