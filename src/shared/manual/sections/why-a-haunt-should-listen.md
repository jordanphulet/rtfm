I have built haunted houses for thirty-one Octobers. I have glued fur to things that should not have fur. I have been bitten by a pneumatic jaw of my own design, which taught me more about pinch points than any manual ever did, this one included. And in all that time the great enemy of the haunt has never been the skeptic, the drunk, or the teenager filming everything on a phone. The great enemy is **the loop**.

## The tyranny of the loop

A traditional animatronic does one thing. A sensor trips, a cylinder fires, a corpse sits bolt upright and shrieks the same recorded shriek it shrieked for the previous four hundred groups. The first group screams. The fortieth group screams a little less, because the fortieth group includes someone who heard about the sitting-up corpse from the first. By the second weekend, guests are *waiting* for it. Some of them are mouthing along.

The loop is why the finest haunts on earth still lean on live actors. An actor notices that the tall one at the back is the one who’s actually scared. An actor hears a guest mutter “it’s just a robot” and answers, softly, “*Then why am I standing behind you?*”

## What listening buys you

A haunt that listens can do what only actors could do before:

- **Answer back.** A performer that hears a taunt and responds to *that* taunt, in character, is a different animal from one that plays clip seven.
- **Pick its audience.** Sensors can tell you who lags behind, who clings, who laughs too loudly. See [Sensing the Guest](section:sensing-the-guest).
- **Change the pacing.** A room that knows the group is already rattled can hold its breath for four more seconds. Silence is the cheapest effect you will ever buy.
- **Back off.** A haunt that can tell genuine panic from delicious fear can turn itself *down*. This matters more than anything else in this book.

That last one is not a footnote. It is the whole philosophy. The language models and robots in these pages exist to make fear more *personal*, and personal fear must be *safe* fear, or it is not entertainment at all — it is an incident report. The promise of this book is three words long: **everyone goes home.** We will wire that promise into steel in [The Show Must Stop](section:the-show-must-stop), and I would rather you read that section twice than once.

## What it costs

Nothing here is free, my dear builder. A listening haunt needs microphones you can trust in a room full of screaming, a model that answers in under a second (we fight that battle in [The Half-Second Problem](section:the-half-second-problem)), and a controller that refuses to let any clever software move a motor to where a hand might be.

> The machine may choose the line. The machine never chooses where the steel goes.

Write that on the wall of your shop. I did. In red. Some say it was paint.
